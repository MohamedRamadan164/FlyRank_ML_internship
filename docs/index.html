Search Intelligence Case Study — Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which pages should a content team refresh first — and how do you know the answer isn't just noise?

A validated, reason-coded scoring pipeline built on real, anonymized Google Search Console data, tested against a plain-English baseline on clients the model has never seen.

FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Repository →
Abstract

Content teams cannot review every page every month, so this work asks which pages are worth reviewing first. A transparent rule-based baseline (stale, still visible, underperforming its position's usual click-through rate) is compared against a logistic-regression model, both evaluated at precision@K on a client-grouped train/test split so results reflect clients the model has never trained on. On a validated 30,000-row prototype sample, the baseline reaches precision@50 of 0.40 against a 0.52 base rate, while the model reaches 0.62 — a meaningful lift using only features knowable before the decision moment, with an explicit leakage trap built and caught along the way. The output is a ranked, reason-coded action queue (refresh / protect / monitor) intended as decision support for a human editor, not an automated verdict.

I.

Introduction

A content team can realistically review a handful of pages a month — never the whole catalog. Left to intuition, that review queue tends to be whatever's top of mind, not necessarily what's actually declining. This project turns that judgment call into something checkable: a ranked list of pages, each with a stated reason, validated against a simple rule anyone could write by hand.

The decision this supports is narrow and human-in-the-loop on purpose: an editor opens the top of the queue and decides whether to rewrite, update, or leave a page alone. Nothing here publishes automatically. A wrong call in either direction has a real but different cost — a false refresh flag wastes an editor's time on a page that was fine; a missed decline goes unnoticed, which is worse, because nothing prompts anyone to look.

II.

Data

The full analysis is built for the FlyRank internship warehouse release (v20260703, ~81.8M rows: a daily performance fact table plus a query table), hosted on Hugging Face and accessed under a gated research-and-education license. The primary tables are dim_content (519,606 rows, one per content item) and fact_content_daily_performance (78.8M rows, one row per content item per day, month-partitioned). Analysis iterates on a single mid-panel month (month=2026-03) rather than the release's final month, which is deliberately held out as a sealed test period rather than used to develop label logic against itself.

Deliberately excluded: the query-level table (its 90-day window overlaps the analysis month's boundary and was not yet aligned for safe use), any row where GA4 engagement data is flagged unavailable rather than genuinely zero, and any deleted content item. All identifiers are salted, hashed pseudonyms used only for joining and grouping — no client name, domain, or raw query appears anywhere in this paper or its underlying code, per the dataset's research-use terms.

Results reported in Section V below are from a smaller, already-public 30,000-row starter sample spanning 32 pseudonymized clients, used to validate the full pipeline end-to-end before the warehouse-scale run. This is stated plainly rather than blended in — see Section VI, Limitations.

III.

Methodology

Label

The outcome is a proxy, not an independently observed future result: a content item is labeled declining if its clicks in the second half of the analysis month were lower than in the first half. This is deliberately simple and stated as a proxy throughout — a stronger version would predict a genuinely future, separate window from past-only features.

Features

Every feature is knowable before the mid-month decision point: static content attributes (word count, content type, backlink count, search volume, competition, content age, days since last update) and first-half-of-month aggregates (clicks, impressions, average search position, a click-through-rate gap against the benchmark for that position tier). Any column used to define the label — second-half clicks, the trend columns from earlier iterations of this work — is explicitly excluded and checked programmatically, not just by eye.

Baseline and validation

The baseline is a rule a non-engineer can read in one sentence: a page is worth reviewing if it hasn't been touched in 90+ days, it's still getting real impressions, and its click-through rate is underperforming what its position tier normally earns. The model is compared against this baseline on an identical client-grouped split — every page from a given client falls entirely in training or entirely in test — so the reported numbers reflect generalization to unseen clients, not memorized client-specific patterns.

Leakage checks

Before any model was trusted, a deliberate leakage trap was built and caught: adding the exact quantity the label is computed from as a feature drove the quick score toward a suspiciously perfect result, confirming the detection would have caught a real mistake — then that column was removed and only the honest number was kept.

IV.

Results

Two signals were checked before any rule was written. Staleness alone was mixed: decline rate rose from 51% (updated in the last 30 days) to 61% (91–180 days), then fell back to 47% at 181+ days — evidence against a simple "older is always worse" rule.

Figure 1. Decline rate by freshness tier. Staleness alone does not move cleanly with decline — a mixed result that shaped the baseline's design.

Click-through rate against position tier was confirmed: weighted CTR falls steadily from the top-3 tier down to deep results, with thousands of rows behind every tier.

Figure 2. Weighted click-through rate by position tier — the confirmed signal behind the scoring rule's CTR-gap term.

The simpler logistic regression matched or beat the random forest at every K — for this question, added complexity did not earn its keep. The top permutation-importance feature was 90-day impression volume, followed by content age and average position; the model's clearest failure mode was flagging recently-published pages (not yet due for review) as if they were neglected, since a fresh page's days_since_last_update looks identical to a genuinely stale one.

V.

Limitations & honest framing

  • The label is a same-window proxy, not an observed future outcome — it does not forecast what a page will do next month.
  • Client history depth varies widely; a single calendar month does not represent equal information for every client in it.
  • A single mid-panel month may not generalize across the full ~17-month panel or across seasons.
  • Small buckets mislead — a 174-row freshness tier reversed the overall trend in this data; any similarly small cut deserves the same suspicion.
  • Nothing here demonstrates that acting on a recommendation causes a traffic or ranking change — there is no experimental design in this data.
  • This model does not predict, infer, or reverse-engineer Google's ranking algorithm. It predicts a proxy label computed from FlyRank's own panel.

Every claim in this paper is observed, directional, and decision-support — never causal, and never a claim about search-engine internals.

VI.

Ranked recommendations

The scoring pipeline outputs three reason-coded actions per content item:

  • Refresh Reason: stale_ctr_underperform. Untouched 90+ days, still visible, underperforming its position tier's usual click-through rate. Highest-priority editor review.
  • Protect Reason: growing_leave_alone. Already well-positioned and trending up — the recommendation is to leave it alone, not intervene.
  • Monitor Reason: ambiguous_watch. No clear signal either way — worth a light periodic check rather than immediate action.

On the prototype sample, this produced 4,129 refresh candidates, 1,401 protect candidates, and 24,470 monitor items out of 30,000 — a shortlist an editor can actually work through, not the whole catalog.
